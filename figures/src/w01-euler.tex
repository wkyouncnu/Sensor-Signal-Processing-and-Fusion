  {\scriptsize\color{soft}

   independent tilts. It is an \textbf{ordered} product of three rotations,

   $\psi = 40^\circ$, $\theta = 20^\circ$, $\phi = 30^\circ$; the dashed square

   readable.}}\\[3.5mm]

\parbox{218mm}{\raggedright\scriptsize
  \textbf{Reading the panels.} The heavy coloured axis in each panel is the one
  the \emph{next} rotation turns about; the coloured arc is a circle drawn in the
  plane perpendicular to it, and the dotted stub joins the arc's centre to that
  axis. An arc whose centre drifts off its axis is the classic way a figure like
  this goes wrong, so the geometry here is computed rather than drawn by
  eye.\\[3pt]
  \textbf{Why the order is fixed.} Rotations do not commute:
  $\mathbf{R}_z\mathbf{R}_y \neq \mathbf{R}_y\mathbf{R}_z$. The convention used
  throughout this course is zyx, so
  $\mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol\Theta) = \mathbf{R}_z(\psi)\mathbf{R}_y(\theta)
  \mathbf{R}_x(\phi)$ — Fossen (2021) \emph{Handbook of Marine Craft
  Hydrodynamics and Motion Control}, 2nd ed., eq.~(2.18), and MSS
  \texttt{Rzyx.m}, which \texttt{\_tools/w01\_euler\_R.m} checks this drawing
  against.\\[3pt]
  \textbf{These are angles, not rates.} The body rates $p$, $q$, $r$ of §1-4 are
  measured about the \emph{body} axes of the last panel only. Turning them into
  $\dot{\boldsymbol\Theta}$ needs the matrix $\mathbf{T}_\Theta$ of §1-5, which is
  \textbf{not} a rotation and is singular at $\theta = \pm 90^\circ$ — the reason
  a surface craft is safe with Euler angles and a submersible may not be.}
